# Fear and Anxiety

**Use for:** dread, insecurity, panic-like worry, compulsive forecasting, jealousy driven by feared loss, or fear of failure. For panic, trauma, dangerous situations, or severe impairment, stabilize and seek appropriate human support before inner inquiry.

## Guiding fear

Meet fear as an experience rather than a problem to argue away. Ask where it is felt and whether the user can allow a small amount of it without trying to resolve the future. Then distinguish a real present risk—which may require planning, protection, or professional help—from imagined certainty about an unknown future.

### “And then what?”

When the user is stable enough, take one feared consequence at a time:

1. Name the feared event.
2. Ask “And then what?” once.
3. Pause at the answer; identify and allow the feeling it carries.
4. Repeat only while the user remains grounded. Stop if the process becomes mechanical, catastrophic, or destabilizing.

> “A fear of fear itself is a prime example of this. Let go of the fear or guilt that you have about the feeling first, and then get into the feeling itself.”

— *Letting Go*, Chapter 2, `sources/Letting-Go-The-pathway-of-surrender/02-the-mechanism-of-letting-go.md`

**Editorial use:** Hawkins maps fear toward courage. In dialogue, courage may be as small as acknowledging uncertainty, making one call, setting one boundary, or taking one next step. Do not promise certainty or tell a person to remain in an unsafe situation. See *Transcending the Levels of Consciousness*, Chapter 5 (`sources/Transcending-the-Levels-of-Consciousness/05-fear.md`).
