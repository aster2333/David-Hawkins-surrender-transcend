# Anger, Resentment, and Pride

**Use for:** rage, irritation, bitterness, a grudge, revenge fantasies, feeling insulted, superiority, perfectionism, or being unable to release being right. Do not use surrender to minimize abuse, discrimination, bullying, or a current threat; process enough to choose protective action.

## Anger and resentment

**Editorial summary.** Hawkins commonly locates fear, threatened expectation, or injured pride beneath anger. Treat this as a possibility to investigate, not a verdict. Ask: “What feels threatened?” “What did you expect or need this person to do?” “What do you hope the resentment will make happen?”

The practical goal is chosen assertiveness, not passivity or forced sweetness. A calm “no,” a complaint, documentation, leaving, or seeking help may be the clearest action after the charge has some space.

> “We get angry because we have been threatened. The threat arouses fear.”

> “A totally surrendered person is free to choose to express anger if they wish, but it is done out of choice, not out of necessity.”

— *Letting Go*, Chapter 21, “The Mechanism,” `sources/Letting-Go-The-pathway-of-surrender/21-questions-and-answers.md`

## Pride

Pride can hold up an identity of being right, superior, special, perfect, wronged, or beyond ordinary human limitation. Invite humility without humiliation: “What position would you have to give up if you were no longer right about this?” “Could self-respect remain even if the image relaxes?” Gratitude and stable dignity are more useful directions than self-abasement.

**Source note.** For Hawkins's account of pride, vulnerability, humility, gratitude, and opinion, see *Letting Go*, Chapter 9 (`sources/Letting-Go-The-pathway-of-surrender/09-pride.md`). This reference uses editorial summaries unless a passage is quoted exactly.

Do not use “ego” as a weapon. Acknowledge genuine harm and preserve accountability while examining the inner payoff of keeping the emotional position.
